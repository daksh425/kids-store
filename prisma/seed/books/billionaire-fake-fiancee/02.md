# Sixty Days

The letter said sixty days.

It said it politely, in the way that only very expensive lawyers can be polite while ruining someone’s life. *Rathore Group Developments has acquired the property at 14 Sea Lane… redevelopment of the site… tenants are requested to vacate the premises… sixty (60) days from the date of this notice.*

The date of the notice was twenty-three days ago.

Tara read it four times at the café table while Zoya stood behind her, reading over her shoulder and making small noises of outrage like a kettle coming to the boil.

“Thirty-seven days,” Tara said finally. “I have thirty-seven days.”

“You have a lawyer,” Zoya said.

“I have a cousin who did one year of law before he became a DJ.”

“You have a petition. We’ll do a petition. The regulars will sign. Mrs D’Souza will sign twice, she signs everything twice.”

“Zoya, they own the building. You can’t petition a building.”

Zoya sat down opposite her and took both her hands, the way she had on the day of Tara’s father’s funeral, when the shop had been closed for the first time in nineteen years and Tara had sat in this exact chair and not cried because she didn’t know how to start.

“Then we find another place,” Zoya said.

They both knew there wasn’t one. Not in Bandra, not at a rent a bookshop could survive. Her father had signed the lease when the building was falling apart and nobody wanted it. Now the lane had a juice bar with a waiting list and a boutique selling linen shirts for the price of a small car. Somewhere in a glass tower, someone had looked at a spreadsheet and seen 14 Sea Lane as a number that could be bigger.

Someone with a serious mouth, who held old books carefully.

Tara picked up the business card and turned it over. On the back, in fountain pen, in handwriting so neat it looked typed, was a mobile number.

*If you change your mind. About anything.*

“Don’t,” Zoya said.

“I’m not going to.”

“You’re going to.”

“I’m going to *think* about it.”

She thought about it all night. She thought about it while she mopped the café floor and emptied the bucket and sat on the shop step with a cup of tea, watching the rain turn the streetlights into long gold smears. She thought about her father painting the sign, his tongue between his teeth, the ampersand coming out lopsided so that he’d laughed and left it that way.

At eleven fifty-two, she sent a message.

*This is the woman who threw chai at your book. We need to talk about my building.*

The reply came eleven seconds later, as if he had been holding the phone.

*It’s my book now. And yes, we do. Tomorrow, 8 a.m. I’ll send a car.*

* * *

The car was the black one from the corner. The driver held an umbrella over her for the four steps from the shop door to the back seat, which was the most anyone had done for Tara in years, and she resented how much she enjoyed it.

The Rathore Group headquarters was forty-two floors of blue glass in Lower Parel, so shiny it reflected the clouds. The lift was silent. The carpet on the top floor was so thick her chappals sank into it. A thin man in round spectacles met her at the lift with a tablet held against his chest like a shield.

“Ms Mehta. I’m Iyer, Mr Rathore’s executive assistant. He’s expecting you.” He looked at her yellow raincoat, then at her face, and his expression did not change at all. “May I take your raincoat?”

“It’s not a raincoat. It’s a personality.”

“I’ll take good care of it,” said Iyer, with immense dignity.

Kabir Rathore’s office was the size of her entire shop. One whole wall was window, and through it Mumbai spread out grey and green and shining wet, all the way to the sea. He was standing at the glass with his back to the door, and when he turned she saw that he looked tired, as if he hadn’t slept either.

On the desk, drying on a white cloth, was the *Gitanjali*.

“You took it,” she said, before she could stop herself.

“You said it wasn’t for sale. I left four thousand rupees under your till.” He paused. “Iyer told me that was the wrong number.”

“It was too much.”

“It was too little. It’s worth more to me than that.” He gestured to a chair. “Please. Sit.”

She sat. He didn’t. He walked to the window and back like a man doing sums.

“Your building,” he said. “I looked into it last night. The redevelopment was approved eight months ago. My cousin Vikram runs Rathore Developments. It’s his project. Luxury residences, forty floors, a rooftop pool.”

“A rooftop pool,” Tara repeated. “Instead of a bookshop that’s been there twenty-two years.”

“I didn’t say it was a good idea.”

“You didn’t say it wasn’t.”

He stopped walking. “I can help you,” he said.

Tara waited. In her experience, when a rich man said *I can help you*, the next word was *if*.

“If,” said Kabir Rathore, “you can help me.”

* * *

He explained it the way he probably explained mergers: clearly, quickly, without looking at her.

His grandmother, Savitri Rathore, had built the company with his grandfather out of a single steel workshop in 1968. She still held the largest block of shares. In six weeks she would turn eighty, and on the night of her birthday she would announce who she was backing as the next chairman of the Rathore Group board.

“You,” Tara said.

“Or Vikram.”

“And she prefers Vikram?”

“She prefers neither of us.” He finally looked at her. “She thinks I’ve become—her word—a glass tower. Cold. All work. No life. She says a man who can’t build a home shouldn’t be trusted to build a company. Vikram has been very helpful in reminding her of that. Vikram is very charming at family dinners.”

“And?”

Kabir was quiet for a moment.

“And last night at dinner,” he said, “after I left your shop, Vikram made a joke about how the only thing I’d ever committed to was a quarterly report. And my grandmother laughed, and then she looked at me in a way she hasn’t looked at me since I was twelve years old. Sort of… disappointed and sad at the same time. And I said something I shouldn’t have said.”

Tara had a horrible feeling she knew where this was going. “What did you say?”

“I said I was engaged.”

The rain ticked against the enormous window.

“To whom?” Tara asked.

“I didn’t say. I said I’d bring her to lunch on Sunday.” He rubbed his forehead, and for a moment he looked less like a billionaire and more like a man who had made a terrible mistake at a dinner table, which, she supposed, he was. “I have never in my life said anything that stupid.”

“And you want—” She couldn’t finish the sentence. It was too absurd.

“I want you to be my fiancée,” said Kabir Rathore. “For six weeks. Until my grandmother’s birthday. And in exchange, 14 Sea Lane stays exactly as it is. Your shop gets a ten-year lease at your current rent, in writing, signed before Sunday.”

Tara stared at him.

Then she started to laugh, and once she had started she could not stop, and she laughed until she had to wipe her eyes on the sleeve of her kurta, while the richest man she had ever met stood by his window and waited, patient and serious and very slightly pink around the ears.

“Why me?” she managed at last. “You must know a thousand women who would—who own the right shoes. Who know which fork to use.”

“That’s exactly why,” he said. “Everyone I know wants something from me. You were the only person in a year who looked at me like I was just a man dripping on your mat.”

“I do want something from you,” Tara pointed out. “I want my shop.”

“Yes,” he said. “But you said it to my face.”
