# Chapter & Chai

For three days, Chapter & Chai was closed.

It had never been closed for three days. Not when the roof fell in during the 2005 floods, not when her father was in hospital, not for anything except the funeral. Zoya came every morning and knocked, and Tara called through the door that she had a cold, and Zoya said *you don’t have a cold, you have a heart*, and left banana cake on the step.

On the third evening, someone knocked who was not Zoya.

Tara knew the knock: three precise taps, evenly spaced, like a clock. She opened the door a crack. Iyer stood under the dripping awning in a dark suit and round spectacles, holding a neatly folded bundle of yellow plastic.

“Your raincoat,” he said. “You left it at the office on the first day. I have been keeping it safe.”

“It’s a personality,” said Tara, automatically.

“I remember.” He held it out. She took it. He did not leave.

They stood looking at each other across the gap in the door. The rain dripped off the awning between them in a silver curtain.

“Ms Mehta,” said Iyer. “I have worked for Mr Rathore for nine years. I am not, by nature, a person who interferes. I believe that a good assistant should be like a good umbrella: present when needed, and otherwise folded away.” He adjusted his spectacles. “However.”

Tara waited.

“However,” said Iyer, “for three days, Mr Rathore has not been to the office. He has cancelled eleven meetings, including one with the Prime Minister’s economic adviser, which I had to reschedule personally, and which was not pleasant. He has not answered his phone. His grandmother informs me that he has been sitting in his mother’s library since Saturday night, reading. Every book. In order. He is currently on the second shelf.”

“Why are you telling me this?”

“Because,” said Iyer, “I have also been in that office every day for nine years, and I have watched a great many people try to get something from him. Money, jobs, introductions, favours. In nine years, you are the only person who ever brought him dinner.” He paused. “Vada pav, I believe. The guard mentioned it. He was very moved.”

Tara’s throat went tight.

“He said it was a deal,” she said.

“Mr Rathore says a great many things,” said Iyer, “in meetings. I have learned to listen to what he does.” He inclined his head, very slightly, a small formal bow. “Goodnight, Ms Mehta. I hope you feel better from your cold.”

He turned and walked away down the wet lane, very straight, without an umbrella, and Tara stood in the doorway holding her yellow raincoat until he had turned the corner.

* * *

On the fourth day, Tara opened the shop, because the regulars had started leaving notes. Mrs D’Souza left two.

She didn’t look at the noticeboard. Somebody had taken the engagement cutting down. Underneath, the eviction letter was gone too. In its place was a single white envelope, thick and cream-coloured, with no logo at all, and her name on the front in handwriting so neat it looked typed.

She didn’t open it. She put it in the drawer under the till and sold a dictionary to a schoolboy and three detective novels to a retired judge and tried not to think.

* * *

Dadi came on the fifth day.

She came in the black car, with the driver holding an umbrella, and she walked into Chapter & Chai leaning on her stick and looked around at the shelves and the leak and the bucket and the battered café tables with the frank, assessing gaze of a woman who had built a company out of a single workshop.

“So,” said Savitri Rathore. “This is the famous shop.”

Tara couldn’t speak. She came round the counter and bent to touch the old woman’s feet, and Dadi let her, and then took her by both arms and pulled her upright.

“Sit,” said Dadi. “Make me chai. Not too sweet.”

Tara made chai. Her hands were shaking so badly that she spilled some on the counter, and for one terrible second she thought of poem thirty-five, and nearly laughed, and nearly cried, and did neither.

Dadi drank half the glass before she said anything.

“I knew,” she said.

Tara stared at her.

“From the first lunch,” said Dadi. “I’m eighty, beta, not foolish. A boy who has never mentioned a single girlfriend in his life suddenly has a fiancée in four days? And she’s a bookseller from Sea Lane, where his cousin is putting up a tower? I knew by the time the dal was served.”

“Then why—” Tara’s voice cracked. “Why did you give me the ring?”

The old woman put down her glass.

“Because when you told me about the book,” she said, “and the chai, and the rain, he was looking at you. And I have not seen my grandson look at anything like that since he was twelve years old, lying on the floor of his mother’s library.” She reached across the table and tapped Tara’s bare finger with one knobbly knuckle. “Fake engagements don’t make men eat 1971 pickle, Tara. And they don’t make them go back into rooms they’ve been afraid of for twenty years.”

Tara looked down at the table.

“He said it was a deal,” she whispered.

“He said it *started* as a deal,” said Dadi. “You left before the rest. Shall I tell you the rest?”

Tara nodded. She couldn’t do anything else.

“He said,” Dadi went on, very slowly, as if she were reading it off a page, “*It started as a deal. And then it stopped being one, and I didn’t say anything, because I was afraid she didn’t feel the same. If anyone in this family needs to be punished for it, it should be me. Give the board to Vikram. Give the company to Vikram. I don’t care. I’d rather have the bookshop.*” She sniffed. “Then he walked out of my birthday party, in front of two hundred people, to run down the hill after you in the rain. Very dramatic. Very unlike him. His grandfather would have been proud.”

Tara put her face in her hands.

“And the board?” she said through her fingers.

“I gave it to Kabir, of course.” Dadi sounded faintly offended. “Vikram stood up at my birthday and humiliated a guest in my garden to win a vote. Kabir stood up and gave the whole thing away for a girl who throws chai at books. Which one would you trust with a company?” She picked up her glass again. “Also, I have cancelled the tower. My building. My decision. Sea Lane is not getting a rooftop pool.”

* * *

He came at seven.

He didn’t come in. He stood outside in the rain, under the tin awning, on the welcome mat, dripping. He wasn’t wearing a suit. He was wearing an old grey kurta and jeans, and he was holding a faded blue book against his chest to keep it dry.

Tara opened the door. The bell gave its tired little *ting*.

“You’re dripping on my welcome mat,” she said.

“I know.” His voice was rough, as if he hadn’t used it much in five days. “I’m browsing. Cautiously.”

“Did you read the letter?”

“Did *you* read the letter?”

She hadn’t. She went to the till and took the cream envelope out of the drawer, and opened it, with Kabir standing in the doorway and Dadi pretending very hard to read a magazine at the café table and Zoya, who had appeared from nowhere, pretending to dust a shelf that didn’t exist.

Inside was a lease. Not ten years. Ninety-nine. Signed, and countersigned, and stamped. And folded inside it was a white paper napkin, smoothed flat, with five rules on it in two kinds of handwriting.

Someone had crossed out Rule One. Underneath, in neat, typed-looking letters, he had written:

**Rule One (amended). Too late.**

Tara looked up.

He had come in from the doorway. He was standing in front of her, very close, rainwater running off his hair onto the floor, holding the *Gitanjali* open at poem thirty-five, at the stain shaped like a perfect brown ring.

“I don’t have a ring,” he said. “My grandmother has it. She says I have to earn it back.” He took a breath. “So I thought I’d start with the truth. It started as a deal. It stopped being one somewhere around the pickle. And I have never, in my whole life, wanted anything as much as I want to sit in this shop every evening at seven, for the next ninety-nine years, and read at the table, and be annoying about it.”

Tara looked at the napkin. She looked at the stain on poem thirty-five. *Where the mind is without fear*, it said, *and the head is held high.*

She held her head high.

“Rule Two,” she said.

He looked at her, and lifted his eyebrows, very slightly. The look. The one that meant *absolutely necessary.*

“In front of witnesses,” Tara said, because Zoya had stopped pretending to dust and Dadi had stopped pretending to read, and three customers and the retired judge had all frozen in the poetry section.

“Absolutely necessary,” said Kabir.

And this time, when he kissed her, nobody was counting, and nobody was watching the board, and the only applause, eventually, came from Mrs D’Souza, who had just walked in from the rain, and who clapped twice.
