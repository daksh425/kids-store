# Rules on a Napkin

They wrote the rules on a napkin, because Tara refused to sign anything she couldn’t read, and the contract Iyer brought in was forty pages long and used the word *hereinafter* nine times on the first page alone.

“Lawyers can do the lease,” she said. “But the rest of it, the *us* part, we do like normal people.”

“Normal people don’t do this.”

“Normal people write things on napkins. Give me a pen.”

They sat at the end of his enormous glass desk, and Iyer brought two cups of coffee and a plate of biscuits that had clearly been chosen from a catalogue, and Tara smoothed a white paper napkin flat between them.

**Rule One,** she wrote. **Nobody falls in love.**

Kabir read it upside down. “Agreed.”

“You said that very fast.”

“I’m a decisive person.”

**Rule Two,** she wrote. **No kissing, unless absolutely necessary in front of witnesses.**

He considered this. “Define necessary.”

“If your grandmother is watching and it would look strange if we didn’t.”

“And who decides when it would look strange?”

“We both do. With a look.”

“What kind of look?”

Tara demonstrated a look. Kabir looked back at her with no expression whatsoever, and after a second she realised he was trying very hard not to laugh.

“That,” he said, “looks like you’re about to sneeze.”

“Then you’ll have to learn it.”

**Rule Three. It ends on the night of the birthday party. A clean, friendly break-up. Nobody is the villain.**

**Rule Four. The shop stays, whatever happens.**

He took the pen from her then, and in his neat, typed-looking handwriting he added:

**Rule Five. Nobody gets hurt.**

Tara looked at that one for a while.

“That’s not a rule,” she said. “That’s a wish.”

“I’m a very good negotiator,” said Kabir. “I get most of what I wish for.”

He folded the napkin into quarters and put it in the inside pocket of his jacket, next to his heart, which was, Tara thought, either very romantic or very good business practice, and she couldn’t tell which.

* * *

They had four days to become a couple.

“How did we meet?” Kabir asked on the first evening, sitting at the café table in Chapter & Chai after closing. He had taken off his jacket and rolled his sleeves up, and he still looked like a magazine advertisement for a watch.

“We should keep it close to the truth,” Tara said. “Lies are easier to remember when they’re mostly true. You came in from the rain, I spilled chai on a book, you were very annoying about it.”

“I was not annoying.”

“You browsed cautiously.”

“And then?”

“And then you kept coming back.” She shrugged. “To apologise for being annoying.”

“How long ago?”

“Four months. Long enough to be serious. Short enough that your grandmother hasn’t met me.”

“Why hasn’t she met you?”

“Because I’m very private. And you’re very busy. And you were afraid she would like me more than you.”

Kabir opened his mouth, closed it again, and wrote *four months* in a small leather notebook.

They went through it all, like revision before an exam. Her favourite colour (the green of the sea before a storm), his (he didn’t have one, which she found deeply suspicious, so she assigned him navy blue). Her birthday, his birthday. The name of her school, the name of his. That she hated coriander, that he was allergic to cats, that she couldn’t whistle, that he had broken his arm at nine falling out of a jamun tree at his grandmother’s house in Lonavala.

“You climbed trees?”

“I was nine. Everyone climbs trees.”

“I can’t imagine you nine.”

“I was very short,” he said gravely, “and wore glasses, and read encyclopaedias under the table at dinner.”

Tara could imagine that, suddenly and completely, and something in her chest gave a small, unwelcome squeeze.

* * *

On the second evening, Zoya made them practise holding hands.

“You hold hands like you’re both being arrested,” she announced from behind the counter. “Relax your fingers. Kabir, you’re not shaking on a deal. Tara, stop looking at his hand like it might bite.”

“This is ridiculous,” said Tara.

“This is your *shop*,” said Zoya. “Hold the billionaire’s hand properly.”

So Tara laced her fingers through his, and his hand was warm and dry and surprisingly steady, and when she looked up he was looking down at their hands as though they were a problem he hadn’t expected to enjoy solving.

“Better,” Zoya said. “Now look at each other like you’ve just remembered something nice.”

They looked at each other. Tara thought of the first rain of the year, and Kabir thought of whatever billionaires thought of, and somewhere in the middle Zoya made a small, smug sound and went to put the kettle on.

* * *

On the third day, he sent a dress.

It arrived in a flat white box tied with silk ribbon, carried by Iyer himself, who handed it over as though delivering a treaty.

“Mr Rathore wishes me to say that you don’t have to wear it,” Iyer said. “He also wishes me to say that his grandmother notices shoes.”

Inside the box was a sari in soft green silk, the exact colour of the sea before a storm, with a thin border of silver thread, and a pair of silver sandals in her size, which she had never told him.

There was a card. *Navy blue is very boring. —K.*

Tara sat down on the shop step with the box on her knees and did not know what to feel, so she felt several things at once and then went back inside and sold three copies of a cookbook to a man who didn’t own a stove.

* * *

On the fourth night, the night before lunch, he walked her home from the shop.

It wasn’t far, just three lanes and a staircase, and the rain had stopped for once, leaving the air warm and heavy and smelling of wet earth. They passed the juice bar and the linen boutique and the little temple on the corner where an old woman was lighting the evening lamp.

“Can I ask you something?” Tara said.

“You’ve asked me eleven hundred things this week.”

“Why the *Gitanjali*? In my shop. You went straight to it.”

He was quiet for half a lane.

“My mother read it to me,” he said finally. “Every night when I was small. She said poetry was like vitamins: you didn’t have to understand it to be good for you. She died when I was twelve. After that my father closed her library. Locked the door. He said it was too painful to look at.” He shrugged, a small stiff movement. “I haven’t been inside it in twenty years.”

Tara didn’t say *I’m sorry.* She’d heard it so many times after her own father, and it never helped. Instead she said, “My father used to read the newspaper out loud to the customers. Even the boring parts. Especially the boring parts. He did voices for the stock market.”

Kabir actually laughed, a short, surprised sound, like a door opening somewhere.

They stopped at the bottom of her staircase. Upstairs, her neighbour’s television was playing a cricket match, and someone had just been bowled out, to loud general despair.

“Tomorrow,” Kabir said. “Twelve-thirty. I’ll pick you up.”

“I’ll be the one in green.”

He looked at her for a moment longer than was strictly necessary for a business arrangement.

“I know,” he said, and walked away into the dark before she could decide what that meant.
