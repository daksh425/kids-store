# Absolutely Necessary

The ballroom of the Sea Palace Hotel looked like the inside of a chandelier.

Everything glittered: the crystal, the cutlery, the women, several of the men. Waiters in white jackets carried trays of tiny food that seemed to have been assembled with tweezers. A string quartet in the corner was playing a classical version of a Bollywood song from the nineties, and nobody except Tara seemed to find this funny.

“Don’t laugh,” Kabir murmured, steering her through the crowd with one hand light against her back.

“They’re playing a film song about a train.”

“Mr Kapadia on the board chose it. He’s very proud of it.”

“Which one is Mr Kapadia?”

“The one waving at us. Smile.”

She smiled. It turned out she was very good at it. She smiled at Mr Kapadia and Mrs Kapadia, at a woman who had won a national award for architecture, at a cricket commentator, at a minister’s wife who asked where Tara’s family was from and then said, “Ah, Pune,” in a tone that made Pune sound like a medical condition.

Through all of it, Kabir stayed beside her. He introduced her to everyone as *my fiancée, Tara, who owns the best bookshop in Bandra*, every single time, the whole sentence, never just *my fiancée.* When the minister’s wife said *Ah, Pune*, his hand found hers and squeezed it, very slightly, and when they had moved away he leaned down and said, close to her ear, “I once saw her eat a samosa with a knife and fork.”

Tara laughed so suddenly that three people turned to look.

“There,” said Kabir, satisfied. “Now they’ll believe it.”

* * *

After the soup, Dadi sent for them.

She sent Iyer, actually, who appeared at Kabir’s elbow like a well-dressed ghost and said, “Your grandmother requests that you dance,” in the tone of a man reading out a court order.

“We don’t dance,” said Kabir.

“Your grandmother anticipated that response,” said Iyer. “She asked me to remind you that she is turning eighty in a month and may not live to see you dance at your own wedding, and that she has asked the quartet to play her song.”

The quartet, on cue, began a slow, sweet melody from a film so old it was in black and white. Around the ballroom, couples who had been married for forty years stood up and drifted onto the floor.

Kabir closed his eyes briefly. “She is going to live to a hundred and twelve,” he said, “purely out of spite.”

But he held out his hand.

Tara had never danced with anyone who wasn’t a cousin at a wedding. She put one hand on his shoulder, and he put one hand at her waist, very lightly, and they stood there for a moment not moving, like two people waiting for a bus.

“You have to actually move,” she whispered.

“I’m aware.”

“Your grandmother is watching.”

“I’m aware of that too.”

He moved. He was terrible. He was stiff and careful and counted under his breath, and twice he stepped on the hem of her sari, and once he turned the wrong way entirely so that they nearly collided with Mr and Mrs Kapadia, who were gliding past like swans.

Tara started to giggle. She couldn’t help it. She put her forehead against his shoulder to hide it, and felt his chest shaking too, and realised he was laughing, silently, helplessly, the way you laugh in a temple or a library when you absolutely must not.

“I told you,” he said into her hair, “I don’t dance.”

“You’re doing very well.”

“I’ve trodden on you three times.”

“Four. But it’s fine. They’re borrowed sandals.”

By the time the song ended they had stopped trying to dance at all, and were just swaying, slightly out of time, holding each other up. When Tara lifted her head, the ballroom seemed very bright, and very far away, and Kabir was looking down at her with that expression she still did not have a name for.

At the head table, Savitri Rathore was applauding, slowly, with her hands held high so that everyone could see.

* * *

Vikram found them at the dessert table.

“Cousin,” he said. “Tara. You look extraordinary.” He took a small chocolate thing from a silver tray and examined it. “I have to admit, I underestimated you both. The newspapers adore you. Dadi adores you. Even Mr Kapadia adores you, and he doesn’t adore anyone.”

“Thank you,” said Tara.

“It’s just funny,” Vikram went on, in his warm, easy voice, “because Kabir has always been so careful. Never dates. Never takes a holiday. Never does anything unplanned. And then one day he walks into a bookshop and—what was it? Four months?”

“Four months,” said Kabir.

“Four months.” Vikram popped the chocolate into his mouth. “And Sea Lane,” he said, still chewing. “Your shop’s on Sea Lane, isn’t it, Tara? Number fourteen?”

The music seemed to get very loud.

“It is,” said Tara.

“What a coincidence,” said Vikram. “That’s one of mine. We’re putting up a lovely tower there. Rooftop pool.” He smiled at her, all teeth. “I suppose that’ll be awkward now. Unless someone’s found a clever solution.”

Kabir’s hand tightened on hers.

“My fiancée’s shop,” he said, and his voice had gone cool and flat, the voice he must use in boardrooms, “is not up for discussion tonight.”

“Of course not,” said Vikram pleasantly. “Tonight’s about love.”

He raised his glass of juice to them. Then he looked past them, over their shoulders, and his smile grew.

“Speaking of which,” he said. “Dadi’s watching.”

Tara turned her head.

At the head table, under the largest chandelier, Savitri Rathore sat very straight in a white silk sari, her stick across her knees. Around her, the board members were talking and laughing. She wasn’t. She was looking directly at the two of them, with an expression that was half fond and half something else, something sharp and questioning.

Beside her, Mr Kapadia was tapping his glass with a spoon. The string quartet stopped. Three hundred people turned towards the head table.

“Friends,” said Mr Kapadia, beaming, “before dessert, a toast. To young love. To Kabir and Tara!”

“To Kabir and Tara!” said three hundred people, and raised three hundred glasses, and looked at them.

And kept looking at them.

There was a pause. In the pause, somebody at the back of the room began to clap, slowly, in rhythm. Somebody else joined in. Mr Kapadia, delighted, started a chant that Tara recognised from every wedding she had ever been to, a chant that only had one possible ending.

Tara looked up at Kabir.

He looked down at her. And she understood, from the slight lift of his eyebrows, that this was it: the look. The one they had practised. The one that meant *in front of witnesses, absolutely necessary.*

It looked nothing like a sneeze.

“Rule Two,” he said, so quietly only she could hear.

“Rule Two,” she agreed.

He bent his head and kissed her.

It was meant to be brief. It was meant to be the kind of polite, public kiss that people give each other at weddings and airports, a full stop at the end of a sentence. And it began that way. But his hand came up to the side of her face, very gently, as though she were something old and precious that might be damaged by being held too hard, and she forgot about the chandeliers and the minister’s wife and Vikram’s smile, and she forgot to stop.

When they finally pulled apart, the room was applauding. Mr Kapadia was wiping his eyes. The string quartet, after a moment of confusion, launched into a different film song entirely, about a moon.

Kabir looked at her. He seemed to have lost the ability to say anything.

“That,” Tara said, a little breathlessly, “was absolutely necessary.”

“Yes,” he said. “Absolutely.”

Over his shoulder, at the head table, Savitri Rathore was smiling. And beside her, Vikram had taken out his phone, and was typing something with his thumb, very fast.
