# The Locked Library

The power went out on Sea Lane at nine o’clock on Tuesday night, which was exactly the moment Kabir Rathore was standing on a chair in Chapter & Chai, trying to fix the leak in the café ceiling with a roll of tape and the confidence of a man who had never fixed anything in his life.

“I told you to call a roofer,” said Tara, from somewhere in the dark below him.

“Roofers take three weeks.”

“You own a construction company.”

“My cousin owns a construction company. I own a steel company. Steel is not roofing.” A pause. “Also, I can’t see anything. Where are you?”

“Down here. Holding the chair.”

“Are you really?”

“No,” said Tara, “I’m eating banana cake. Of course I’m holding the chair. Come down before you fall and I have to explain to your grandmother that I killed you with a leak.”

He climbed down. In the dark, he misjudged the last step, and she caught his arm, and for a second they stood very close in the warm, rain-smelling blackness of the shop, his hand on her shoulder, her hand on his sleeve.

Then Zoya’s voice came from the front, cheerful and loud: “Candles! I found the candles! Nobody move, there’s a box of books on the floor and I don’t want to sue anyone.”

They let go of each other.

* * *

Zoya lit eleven candles and went home, with a look over her shoulder that Tara pretended not to see.

They sat on the floor of the café with their backs against the counter, because the chairs were wet, and ate the last two slices of banana cake from the box with their fingers. Outside, the whole lane was dark. The rain made a soft, steady roar on the tin awning. Every so often a car went past and its headlights swept across the shelves, lighting up the spines of the books for a second, red and gold and blue, and then left them in shadow again.

“Tell me about your library,” Tara said.

“My mother’s library.”

“Tell me about it.”

He was quiet for a while. She thought he wasn’t going to answer.

“It’s on the top floor of the house,” he said finally. “At the back. It has a round window that looks out over the sea. She had it built the year I was born. Floor to ceiling, two walls of books, a ladder on wheels. A window seat with green cushions. She used to sit there in the afternoons and read, and I used to lie on the floor and read too, and we wouldn’t talk for hours, and it was the best thing in my whole life.” He picked at a crumb on his knee. “After she died my father had the door locked. He said books gather dust and memories gather pain. He was not a poetic man, normally. That was the most poetic thing he ever said.”

“And the key?”

“My grandmother has it. She’s offered it to me every year on my mother’s birthday. I’ve never taken it.”

“Why not?”

He tilted his head back against the counter. In the candlelight, his face looked younger, unguarded.

“Because if I go in,” he said, “it will be real that she’s gone. And if I don’t, it’s like she’s just in there reading, and she’ll come down for dinner.”

Tara didn’t say anything. She reached over, in the dark, and found his hand, and held it. Not the way Zoya had taught them. Just the way you hold someone’s hand.

They sat like that for a long time, while the candles burned down.

* * *

On Saturday morning, her mother called from Pune.

Tara had been dreading this call for two weeks. Her mother did not read the gossip columns, on principle, but her mother’s sister did, and her mother’s sister’s neighbour, and the entire WhatsApp group of the Pune Ladies’ Library Association, which had four hundred members and no sense of mercy.

“So,” said her mother, without saying hello. “I am the last to know.”

“Mumma—”

“The *last*, Tara. Kamla Auntie knew. Kamla Auntie’s *maid* knew. I find out my only daughter is engaged to a steel magnate from a picture on a phone at a kitty party, holding a samosa.”

“Who was holding the samosa?”

“*I* was holding the samosa,” said her mother. “I nearly dropped it.”

Tara sat down on the shop step with the phone against her ear and her eyes closed. Across the lane, the juice bar was setting out its chairs. The sky was a heavy, bruised grey, deciding whether to rain.

“It’s complicated,” she said.

“Engagements are not complicated. Either you love the boy or you don’t.”

Tara opened her mouth to say *it’s not like that*, the way she had said it to Zoya, to herself, to the ceiling at two in the morning. It didn’t come out.

There was a long pause on the line.

“Ah,” said her mother, in an entirely different voice. “I see.”

“You don’t see. There’s nothing to see.”

“Your father,” said her mother gently, “used to go quiet like that too. When I asked him about me. Before.” Another pause. “Is he kind to you?”

Tara thought about it. The umbrella from the shop door to the car. The navy-blue card. *My fiancée, Tara, who owns the best bookshop in Bandra,* every single time, the whole sentence. The way he held old books. The way he’d held her hand in the dark.

“Yes,” she said. “He’s kind.”

“Does he read?”

“At the table,” said Tara. “All the time. It’s very annoying.”

Her mother laughed, the same short surprised laugh that Tara had, the one people always said made them sound alike.

“Then bring him to Pune,” said her mother, “so I can feed him and judge his shoes. And Tara?”

“Mm?”

“Whatever is complicated,” said her mother, “don’t let it be more complicated than it needs to be. Your father nearly lost me because he was too proud to say a simple thing. Say the simple thing.”

She hung up. Tara sat on the step for a long time, the phone in her lap, while the first drops of rain began to fall on the lane.

* * *

On Sunday, he took the key.

Dadi didn’t make a speech. She just took it from a little silver box on her dressing table and put it in his palm and closed his fingers over it, the same way she had given Tara the ring. Then she sat down in her chair by the window, picked up her newspaper, and pretended very hard to read it.

Kabir and Tara climbed the stairs to the top floor of the house together. The corridor at the back was dim and cool. At the end of it was a wooden door, painted green, with a brass handle gone dull with twenty years of not being touched.

Kabir stood in front of it with the key in his hand and didn’t move.

“I can wait outside,” Tara said.

“No,” he said quickly. Then, more quietly, “No. Please.”

He turned the key. It stuck, and then it gave, with a click that seemed very loud.

The door swung open.

The room was full of light. That was the first thing Tara noticed. Someone—Dadi, she would learn later, every month for twenty years—had kept the round window clean, and the afternoon sun poured through it, gold and slanting, full of slowly turning dust. The shelves went up to the ceiling on two walls, just as he’d said. The ladder on its wheels stood where it had last been left. The green cushions on the window seat had faded almost to grey.

On the little table beside the window seat, face-down and open, as if someone had just stepped away to answer the door, was a book.

Kabir walked over to it. He didn’t pick it up. He just looked at it.

“She was reading this,” he said. “The week she went into hospital. She said she’d finish it when she came home.”

Tara came and stood beside him. It was a paperback novel, its spine cracked, a bus ticket from 2006 tucked in at page two hundred and twelve as a bookmark.

“Do you want to finish it for her?” Tara asked.

Kabir made a sound that was half a laugh and half something else entirely. He sat down on the faded window seat, very suddenly, as if his legs had decided for him, and put his face in his hands.

Tara sat down beside him. She didn’t say anything. She put her arm around his shoulders, and he leaned against her, this enormous serious man in his expensive shirt, and the sun moved slowly across the floor, and the dust turned and turned in the light.

After a while, he lifted his head and wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand.

“The books need airing,” Tara said, practical, because one of them had to be. “Some of these will have damp in the spines. And the cushions need replacing. And you’ll want a proper lamp for the evenings, because that bulb is terrible.”

He looked at her.

“Will you help me?” he said.

“With the books?”

“With all of it.”

The ring on her finger, his mother’s ring, caught the light from the round window and threw a tiny spark across the shelves.

*Rule One,* Tara thought. *Nobody falls in love.*

“Yes,” she said.
