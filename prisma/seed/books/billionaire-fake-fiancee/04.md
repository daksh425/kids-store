# Sunday Lunch with Dadi

The Rathore house on Malabar Hill was not a house. It was a small white kingdom.

It sat behind high walls and old trees at the top of a winding road, with a garden that sloped down towards the sea and a veranda lined with pots of jasmine. There were two peacocks on the lawn. Tara was fairly sure the peacocks were judging her.

“Breathe,” Kabir said, as the car stopped.

“I am breathing.”

“You’re breathing like you’re going to sing.”

“You’re one to talk. You haven’t blinked since the flyover.”

He looked at her, and for the first time she saw that he was nervous too, a tiny tightness at the corner of his mouth. Without thinking, she reached over and straightened his collar, which didn’t need straightening.

“It’s just lunch,” she said. “We’re just two people who met in the rain and are very annoying about it.”

He let out a breath. Then he got out, came round, and opened her door, and offered her his hand, and when she took it their fingers laced together exactly the way Zoya had taught them, as if they’d been doing it for years.

* * *

Savitri Rathore was waiting in the drawing room in a cream cotton sari, sitting very straight in a carved chair by the window, with reading glasses on a chain around her neck and a walking stick she clearly used more for pointing than for walking.

She was small. Tara hadn’t expected her to be small. Everything else about her was enormous: her stillness, her gaze, the silence in the room when she lifted one finger and everyone stopped talking.

“So,” said Dadi. “This is the mystery.”

“Dadi, this is Tara Mehta,” Kabir said. “Tara, my grandmother.”

Tara bent and touched her feet, the way her own mother had taught her, and when she straightened up Dadi was looking at her sandals.

“Silver,” said Dadi. “Good. Gold is for people who are trying too hard.” She pointed her stick at the chair beside her. “Sit. Kabir, go away and talk to your cousin. I want her alone.”

“Dadi—”

“*Go.*”

Kabir went. At the door, he looked back at Tara with an expression of such naked alarm that she almost laughed out loud.

* * *

Vikram Rathore arrived ten minutes later, and he was everything Kabir was not.

He was handsome in an easy, golden way, with a wide smile and a linen shirt open at the collar and a voice that made everything sound like the start of a funny story. He kissed Dadi on both cheeks and then, without asking, kissed Tara’s hand.

“The famous fiancée,” he said. “I have to say, I had a bet with myself that you didn’t exist.”

“And?” said Tara.

“And I lost.” He smiled at her, warm and appreciative, and only his eyes didn’t join in. “Where did my serious cousin find you?”

“In the rain,” said Tara. “He ruined a book I loved.”

Vikram laughed. Dadi didn’t. Dadi was watching Tara with an expression that was impossible to read.

“Tell me about the book,” said Dadi.

So Tara told her. Not the deal, not the napkin, not the eviction letter. Just the rain and the leak and the bucket and the glass of chai sailing through the air like a comet, and the faded blue *Gitanjali*, and the stain across poem thirty-five, and the man who had stood there in his expensive suit and said *I’ll buy it ruined.*

By the end, Dadi had taken off her reading glasses.

“His mother had that edition,” the old woman said quietly. “Ananya. She read it to him every night.”

“I know,” said Tara. “He told me.”

Something passed across Dadi’s face then, quick as a bird crossing a window.

“He told you,” she repeated. “He doesn’t tell anyone anything. He hasn’t said her name at this table in twenty years.”

At the far end of the room, Kabir had gone very still.

* * *

Lunch was served on the veranda: dal, fish curry, rice, a mountain of fried bhindi, and a small silver bowl of pickle that Dadi informed her had been made by her own mother in 1971 and was guarded like the crown jewels.

“Kabir doesn’t eat pickle,” said Vikram helpfully. “Do you, cousin? Too messy.”

Kabir, without looking up, reached across the table, took a large spoonful of the 1971 pickle, and ate it.

There was a short, stunned silence.

Then Dadi laughed. It was a real laugh, loud and creaky and delighted, and she slapped the table with her hand so that the spoons rattled.

“Hah!” she said. “Look at that. Look at his face! He’s going to die. Somebody get him water.”

Kabir’s eyes were watering. He was turning slowly red. He held up one finger, as if asking for a moment, and drank an entire glass of water, and Tara, who had been holding her breath, burst out laughing too, and then she couldn’t stop, and she had to put her face in her napkin.

Under the table, his knee bumped hers. When she looked at him, he was smiling. A real one, the kind that went all the way up. She had never seen it before, and she was not prepared for it.

*Rule One,* she reminded herself firmly. *Nobody falls in love.*

* * *

After lunch, while Vikram took a phone call in the garden, Dadi made Tara walk with her along the veranda.

The old woman walked slowly, her hand light on Tara’s arm, her stick tapping. The sea glittered beyond the trees. The peacocks had gone to sulk under a mango tree.

“You run a bookshop,” said Dadi.

“My father’s. Now mine.”

“Is it a good business?”

Tara could have lied. She didn’t. “No,” she said. “But it’s a good place.”

Dadi nodded, as if that were the answer she had been waiting for.

They stopped at the end of the veranda, where a door stood half open into a cool, dim room. Tara glimpsed a long table and old photographs in silver frames: a young couple in front of a workshop, a boy in round glasses holding a cricket bat taller than himself.

Dadi reached into the pocket of her sari and took out something small. She pressed it into Tara’s palm and folded Tara’s fingers over it with her own.

“This was Ananya’s,” she said. “Her engagement ring. My son gave it to me after she died. I have been keeping it for the right person.”

Tara opened her hand.

It was not a big ring. It was a narrow band of old gold with a single small diamond set in a ring of tiny seed pearls, a little worn on one side, as if the person who had worn it had a habit of turning it round and round while she read.

“I can’t,” Tara said. Her voice came out wrong. “Dadi, I can’t take this.”

“You can,” said Savitri Rathore. “And you will. Because he told you about her.” She patted Tara’s hand. “Don’t let him become a glass tower, beta. That is all I ask.”

From the garden, Kabir’s voice was calling her name.

Tara closed her hand around his mother’s ring, and smiled, and walked back across the veranda towards him, feeling like the worst person in Mumbai.
