# Cracks in the Contract

They spent three Sundays in the library.

They took every book off every shelf, one by one, and wiped the covers with soft cloths and stood the damp ones open on the floor in the sun to dry, like a small paper forest. They found pressed flowers in a book of Urdu poetry and a postcard from Shimla dated 1987 in an atlas, and in a fat cookbook, between the pages for biryani, a crayon drawing of a dinosaur signed, in enormous backwards letters, KABIR AGE 5.

“It’s very good,” said Tara gravely, holding it up to the light.

“It’s a cow,” said Kabir.

“It has spikes.”

“It’s an angry cow.”

Dadi came up every afternoon at four with a tray of tea and pretended she was just passing. She sat on the window seat with her stick across her knees and told stories: about Ananya, about the steel workshop in 1968, about the day Kabir’s grandfather had proposed to her at a railway station by accidentally shouting it over the noise of a train, so that half the platform had applauded.

“He was a terrible romantic,” said Dadi. “Like this one.”

“I’m not romantic,” said Kabir, from the top of the ladder.

“He ate the 1971 pickle,” Dadi said to Tara, as if this settled the matter. “For you. In front of everyone. If that’s not romance, I don’t know what is.”

Kabir dropped a book.

* * *

It was in the third week that things began to crack.

It started with small things. A journalist outside the shop who didn’t want to talk about romance, only about property: *Is it true your shop is in a Rathore Developments building, Ms Mehta? Is it true it was scheduled for demolition?* A message from an unknown number that said only *Congratulations on your arrangement* and then nothing else. A photograph that appeared in a gossip column, slightly blurred, of Iyer arriving at Chapter & Chai with a thick legal envelope.

“Vikram,” Kabir said, when she showed him. His face had gone hard. “He’s digging.”

“What will he find?”

“A lease,” said Kabir. “A perfectly ordinary lease, signed by my company, for your shop, at your current rent, for ten years. Dated two days before our engagement.”

Tara sat down.

“Which looks,” she said slowly, “like you paid me.”

“Which looks,” Kabir agreed, “like exactly what it is.”

They looked at each other across the café table. Outside the rain had stopped, and the lane was busy with the evening crowd, and somebody’s radio was playing an old love song.

“It’s fine,” Kabir said. “It’s ten more days. The party is in ten days. After that we break up, nicely, like we planned. Nobody is the villain. Vikram can say what he likes.”

“Your grandmother will know.”

“I’m going to tell her anyway. On the night. I promised.”

“And the board?”

He shrugged, but she saw it, the flicker of something that wasn’t indifference. He had spent ten years building the company. He had spent his whole life trying to be the kind of grandson who deserved a steel workshop and a railway-station proposal and a library with a round window.

“The board,” said Kabir, “will do whatever it does.”

* * *

On Wednesday night, he called from Delhi.

It was nearly midnight. Tara was in bed with a paperback she wasn’t reading, listening to the rain on the window, and when her phone lit up with his name she looked at it for three rings before she answered, so that she wouldn’t seem like a person who had been waiting.

“Did I wake you?”

“No,” she said. “I was reading.”

“What?”

She looked at the cover. She had no idea. “Something with a lighthouse.”

He laughed, low and tired. Behind him she could hear the hum of a hotel air conditioner and, very faintly, traffic.

“How was Delhi?”

“Long. Eleven meetings. A man from a bank explained interest rates to me as if I were a child. I thought of your father doing voices for the stock market and nearly laughed in his face.” A pause. “Tara?”

“Mm?”

“Can I read you something?”

She sat up. “Now?”

“I brought one of my mother’s books. From the library. I hope that’s all right. I’ve been reading it on planes.” She heard pages turning. “It’s Tagore. The one with the stain is safer at home. This is a different edition. It doesn’t smell of ginger.”

“Go on, then,” said Tara.

So he read to her. Not poem thirty-five; a different one, about a traveller who knocks at every door before he finds his own, and has to wander through all the outer worlds to reach the innermost shrine at the end. He read slowly and carefully, in the same voice he used for everything, serious and precise, and somewhere around the third line Tara lay back down on her pillow and closed her eyes and just listened.

When he finished, neither of them said anything for a while.

“My mother used to say,” said Kabir eventually, “that you can tell everything about a person by what they read out loud to you at midnight.”

“And what does that one tell me?”

There was a long silence on the line, full of the hum of the hotel and the distant traffic and the rain on her own window, two cities apart.

“That I’ve been a long time getting home,” he said. “Goodnight, Tara.”

He hung up before she could answer. She lay there in the dark with the phone on her chest, and the lighthouse book slid off the bed onto the floor, and she didn’t pick it up.

* * *

Vikram came into the shop on Thursday, alone, at a time when Kabir was in a meeting in Delhi.

He browsed. Not cautiously. He walked straight past the poetry and the classics and stopped in front of the noticeboard, where the newspaper cutting of their engagement still hung, pinned over a corner of white paper.

He lifted the corner of the cutting with one finger.

“Sixty days,” he read aloud, from the eviction letter underneath. “Dated…” He did the sum in his head, theatrically. “Ah. So you were already counting down when my cousin walked in from the rain. And five days later you were engaged, and your lease was renewed for ten years.” He let the cutting fall back. “Isn’t love wonderful?”

“What do you want, Vikram?” Tara said.

“Want? Nothing. I’m just a man who likes a good story.” He turned and leaned on the counter, very close, smiling his warm, golden smile. “And it is a good story. The lonely billionaire, the struggling bookseller. Very romantic. Very *convenient*.” He picked up a bookmark, looked at it, put it down. “Here’s what I think. I think my cousin bought himself a fiancée to impress our grandmother. And I think you sold yourself for a shop.”

Tara had never wanted to throw chai at anyone more.

“You don’t know anything about me,” she said.

“I know you’re a good actress,” said Vikram. “The kiss at the gala was very convincing. Mr Kapadia cried.” He straightened up. “But I’ve been in that family for thirty-five years, Tara. Kabir doesn’t fall in love. Kabir doesn’t do anything he hasn’t calculated first. Whatever you think is happening, it isn’t. It’s a deal. And on Saturday night, in front of Dadi and the whole board, I’m going to make sure everyone knows exactly what kind of deal it was.”

He smiled at her one more time and walked out, and the bell over the door gave its tired little *ting*.

Tara stood very still behind the counter.

On her finger, the ring had turned round, so that the small diamond and its seed pearls faced her palm. She turned it back with her thumb, the way, she suddenly realised, she had seen Kabir’s mother do it in a photograph in the library: sitting on the window seat with a book, turning her ring round and round while she read.

*Kabir doesn’t fall in love,* Vikram had said. *It’s a deal.*

The worst part, Tara thought, was that he was right.

And the second-worst part was that, somewhere in the last five weeks, between the chai and the pickle and the angry cow, she had stopped caring that it was.
