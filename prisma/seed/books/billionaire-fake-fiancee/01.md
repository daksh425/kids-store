# A Ruined First Edition

The rain arrived in Bandra the way trouble usually did: all at once, without asking, and soaking everything Tara Mehta loved.

She was halfway up the ladder in Chapter & Chai, rescuing a row of poetry paperbacks from the leak above the window, when the bell over the door gave its tired little *ting* and a man walked in out of the monsoon.

He did not shake the water off like a normal person. He stood very still in the doorway, as if the shop had been placed there purely to inconvenience him, and looked back over his shoulder at the street.

Tara looked too. Two men with cameras were jogging past the paan shop opposite, heads swivelling, umbrellas forgotten.

“If you’re hiding,” she said from the ladder, “you’re dripping on my welcome mat.”

He turned. He was tall, in a charcoal suit that had clearly cost more than her shop made in a month, and he had the kind of face that belonged on the business pages: sharp jaw, serious mouth, dark eyes that looked as though they were calculating the square footage of everything they saw.

“I’m not hiding,” he said. “I’m browsing.”

“In the doorway.”

“I browse cautiously.”

Tara climbed down. Up close he smelled of rain and expensive soap, and he was frowning at the shelves as if they had personally disappointed him.

“We have a café at the back,” she said. “Chai is forty rupees. Browsing is free, but the books are not, and the floor is slippery, so please don’t do anything dramatic.”

“I never do anything dramatic.”

He said it with such perfect seriousness that she almost laughed.

* * *

Chapter & Chai had been her father’s idea first and her life second. He had opened it twenty-two years ago in the ground floor of a crumbling four-storey building on Sea Lane, with a hand-painted sign, three shelves and a second-hand kettle. *People will always need two things,* he used to say, *a good story and a hot cup of something.*

The shelves had multiplied. The kettle had been replaced four times. Her father had been gone for three years, and Tara had kept the shop open through stubbornness, overdrafts, and the loyalty of exactly forty-one regular customers, whom she knew by name and preferred biscuit.

The man in the suit was not one of them.

He wandered towards the back, and Tara tried to go back to the leak, but he moved through the shop like a weather system, and her eyes kept following him. He did not look at the bestsellers. He did not glance at the café menu. He went straight to the glass cabinet beside the counter, the one with the small brass lock, and stopped.

“That one,” he said. “May I?”

Inside the cabinet were the few books she couldn’t bring herself to sell cheaply: a set of old Penguin classics with orange spines, a signed poetry collection, and, in the centre, a faded blue hardback of Tagore’s *Gitanjali*, printed in 1913, with a library stamp from a school in Shimla and someone’s pencilled notes in the margins.

“It’s not really for sale,” she said.

“Then why is it in a shop?”

“Because it’s safe there.”

He considered this. “May I hold it? Carefully.”

There was something in the way he said *carefully* that made her unlock the cabinet. She put the book in his hands, and for a moment his whole face changed. The frown went. The square-footage look went. He opened the cover the way people touch something they’ve missed.

“My mother had this edition,” he said quietly. “She wrote in the margins too.”

Tara didn’t know what to say to that, so she did what she always did when a customer went quiet over a book. She went to make chai.

* * *

It was not her fault, exactly.

It was the fault of the roof, which chose that moment to give up on the corner above the café. It was the fault of the bucket she had placed there an hour ago, which was now full. And it was, she would maintain for the rest of her life, at least partly the fault of a man who stood so close to a café table while reading a hundred-year-old book.

The ceiling dripped. The bucket overflowed. Tara, carrying two glasses of ginger chai, stepped into the overflow, slid gracefully across the tiles like a cricketer diving for a catch, and launched both glasses into the air.

One landed on the floor.

The other landed, with the precision of a guided missile, on the open pages of *Gitanjali*.

For a long second, neither of them moved. The rain hammered the window. A drop of chai rolled slowly down the man’s very expensive sleeve.

Then they both moved at once. He lifted the book, she grabbed napkins, their hands collided, more chai splashed, and somehow the book ended up with a perfect brown ring across poem thirty-five.

*Where the mind is without fear and the head is held high,* read the stained page.

Tara’s head was not held high. Tara wanted to crawl under the counter and live there.

“I am so sorry,” she said, dabbing. “I’m so, so sorry. It’s my roof. It’s my bucket. I—”

“It was my fault,” he said.

“It was not your fault. You were standing there being careful.”

“I distracted you.”

“You absolutely did not.” She caught herself. “I mean, you did, but not in a—never mind.”

He looked at the stain, then at her, and something that was almost a smile crossed his serious face. “I’ll buy it.”

“It’s ruined.”

“Then I’ll buy it ruined.” He pulled out a slim leather wallet. “How much?”

“It’s not for sale,” she said, which was a ridiculous thing to say about a book that now smelled of ginger, but it came out anyway.

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he took a business card from the wallet and set it on the counter, very precisely, beside the napkins.

“If you change your mind,” he said, “about anything.”

He handed back the book. He nodded once. And then he walked back out into the rain, past the paan shop and the photographers, into a black car that had been waiting on the corner with its engine running all along.

* * *

Zoya arrived at six for the evening shift, shaking out an umbrella shaped like a frog.

“Why does it smell like a chai stall in here?”

“Don’t ask.”

“Why is the Tagore in the window drying like laundry?”

“I said don’t ask.”

Zoya picked up the business card from the counter, read it, and dropped her frog umbrella on the floor.

“Tara,” she said slowly. “Do you know who this is?”

“A man who browses cautiously.”

Zoya held up the card. It was heavy, cream-coloured, with a single line of engraved silver text.

**KABIR RATHORE**
*Chief Executive, Rathore Group*

“Rathore Group,” Zoya said. “Like, the Rathore Group. Steel, ports, hotels, half the skyline of Lower Parel. Like, the Rathore Group whose name is on the letter you’ve been ignoring on the noticeboard for three weeks.”

Tara’s stomach dropped somewhere around her ankles.

She turned, very slowly, to the noticeboard behind the till. Between the flyer for a children’s story hour and a postcard from her mother in Pune was a stiff white envelope she had pinned up and refused to open, because nothing good ever arrived in an envelope that stiff.

The logo in the corner was a silver R.

She had just thrown chai on a billionaire.

And she was fairly sure he owned her building.
