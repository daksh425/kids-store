# The Eightieth Birthday

Savitri Rathore’s eightieth birthday party was held in the garden of the Malabar Hill house, under a sky that had, for once, decided to be kind.

The monsoon had paused. The lawn had been strung with a thousand small white lights. There were marigolds on every table and a band playing old Hindi songs from the forties and fifties, the songs Dadi had danced to when she was young, and two hundred guests in silk and linen, and the peacocks, who had been shut in the garage for their own safety and were screaming about it.

Tara stood at the edge of the lawn in a sari the colour of moonlight and felt sick.

“Breathe,” said Kabir, appearing at her elbow with two glasses of lime soda.

“I am breathing.”

“You’re breathing like you’re about to sing.”

“That,” said Tara, “was my line.”

He handed her a glass. He looked tired again, the way he had on the first morning in his office, shadows under his eyes. He had flown back from Delhi that afternoon. She hadn’t told him about Vikram’s visit. She hadn’t known how.

“After the cake,” Kabir said quietly, “I’m going to take Dadi into the house and tell her everything. Before her announcement. She should know the truth before she decides anything.”

“And then?”

“And then I’ll walk you to the car, and we’ll break up in the driveway in a dignified and friendly manner, and you’ll keep your shop, and I’ll—” He stopped.

“You’ll what?”

He looked at her for a long moment. The little lights in the trees were reflected in his eyes.

“I’ll take the *Gitanjali* back to the library,” he said, “where it belongs.”

“Kabir,” Tara said. “There’s something I need to tell you. Vikram came to the shop on Thursday, and he—”

“Kabir!” Mr Kapadia had appeared out of the crowd, beaming, with a large plate of kebabs in one hand and a man from a bank in the other. “Kabir, you must meet Mr Sethi, Mr Sethi has been dying to meet you, Mr Sethi has a fascinating idea about shipping containers—”

Kabir looked at Tara. *Wait,* his face said. *Two minutes.*

It was not two minutes. It was twenty. Mr Sethi’s idea about shipping containers turned out to have several parts. By the time Kabir escaped, Dadi had sent Iyer to fetch Tara to meet a row of elderly aunties who wished to inspect her, one by one, like a jeweller inspecting a stone. They asked about her family, her horoscope, her cooking, and whether bookshops made any money, and Tara answered every question politely and watched Kabir across the lawn the whole time, and he watched her back.

Once, he started towards her. A cousin intercepted him with a baby that needed to be photographed with its famous uncle.

Once, she started towards him. Zoya, who had been invited as Tara’s plus-one and was having the night of her life, grabbed her arm and dragged her to the dance floor, where a cluster of teenage Rathores were attempting a dance from a music video.

“Zoya, I need to talk to him.”

“You can talk to him for the rest of your life,” said Zoya, “this song is three minutes,” and spun her round.

And then the band stopped, and the lights in the trees flickered and dimmed, and two waiters wheeled out the cake.

* * *

The cake was four tiers of white and gold, with eighty candles that took three nephews and a cousin to light. Dadi blew them out in one go, with tremendous force, and then pointed her stick at the crowd and said, “Not one of you could do that,” and everybody cheered.

And then Vikram stood up and tapped his glass.

“Before Dadi makes her announcement,” he said, smiling, into the band’s microphone, “I wanted to say a few words. About family. About honesty.”

Kabir went very still beside her.

“We all love a good love story,” Vikram said. “And this year, we were given a wonderful one. My cousin Kabir, who we all thought was married to his work, found love in a little bookshop in Bandra. In the rain. Isn’t that beautiful?”

A murmur of agreement. A few people looked over at them and smiled. Tara’s face felt frozen.

“There’s only one small problem with the story,” said Vikram, and he held up a sheet of paper. “It isn’t true.”

The garden went quiet.

“This,” said Vikram, “is a ten-year lease for a shop at 14 Sea Lane, owned by Rathore Group. Signed by Kabir. For the bookshop of Ms Tara Mehta. Dated the same week they met. Two days before they told Dadi they were engaged.” He turned the paper so that the people in the front row could see it. “I don’t think that’s a love story. I think that’s a contract. I think my cousin hired himself a fiancée, to win Dadi’s vote, and paid her with a building.”

Somebody gasped. Someone else laughed, nervously, and stopped. Two hundred faces turned towards Tara. Then they turned towards Dadi, who sat at the head table with her hands folded over the top of her stick and her face like carved stone.

Kabir stepped forward.

“That’s enough, Vikram.”

“Is it true?” Vikram said.

“That’s *enough*.”

“It’s a simple question, cousin. Yes or no.” Vikram held the paper out towards him. “Is it a deal?”

And Kabir, who had never told a lie at that family table until five weeks ago, and who was standing in front of the grandmother he loved more than anyone alive, looked at the lease in his cousin’s hand and said:

“It started as a deal.”

* * *

Tara didn’t hear what he said next.

She heard *it started*, and *a deal*, and then the blood was rushing in her ears so loudly that the band and the crowd and the screaming peacocks all disappeared, and she was standing in the middle of a fairy-lit garden in a borrowed sari, with two hundred people looking at her as if she were a thief.

Which she was, she supposed. She had taken a ring that wasn’t hers. She had taken an old woman’s trust. She had taken a lease. She had taken five weeks of Sunday afternoons in a library with a round window, and angry cows, and banana cake in the dark, and she had let herself believe, for a few stupid, hopeful days, that some of it might be real.

*It started as a deal.*

Of course it had. That was the rule. She had written it herself, on a napkin.

She turned and walked across the lawn towards the house. Somebody called her name. She didn’t stop.

In the cool, dim hallway, she pulled the ring off her finger. It came off easily, as if it had never fitted at all. She put it on the little table by Dadi’s chair, beside the silver box where the library key had been kept, and she left the green silk sari folded on the chair in the guest room where she’d changed, with the silver sandals on top, and she walked out of the side gate in her own chappals and down the long hill in the dark.

Her phone buzzed in her bag. *Kabir.* She didn’t answer.

It buzzed again. And again. Seven times, on the way down the hill. Then a message.

*Please. You didn’t hear the rest.*

Tara turned the phone off.

At the bottom of the hill, the rain came back, all at once, without asking, and soaked everything she loved.
