# A Ring That Fits

In the car, Tara opened her hand and showed him.

Kabir looked at the ring for a long time. The car went down the hill, round the bends, past the hanging gardens and the joggers and the vendors selling roasted corn, and he didn’t say anything at all.

“I tried to give it back,” she said. “She wouldn’t let me.”

“No,” he said. “She wouldn’t.”

“We have to tell her. We can’t let her give me your mother’s—Kabir, this is wrong. This is a real thing. It’s not a prop.”

“If we tell her now,” he said, very quietly, “Vikram gets the board, the tower goes up on Sea Lane, and my grandmother finds out the only time I told her I was happy, I was lying.” He rubbed his thumb across his knuckles. “I’ll give it back to her myself. On the night of the party. I’ll tell her everything. I promise.”

Tara looked at the ring.

“Put it on,” he said. “Please. She’ll ask.”

She slid it onto her finger. It went over the knuckle and settled into place as if it had been made for her, which was absurd, because it had been made for a woman who had died twenty years ago, and Tara’s fingers were famously difficult. Even bangles hated her.

They both looked at it.

“It fits,” Kabir said, in a strange voice.

“Lots of rings fit people,” said Tara.

“Not that one. My mother had very small hands. My grandmother tried it on every one of my cousins’ wives. It didn’t fit anyone.”

Tara put her hand in her lap, under her pallu, where neither of them had to look at it.

“It’s just a ring,” she said.

“Yes,” said Kabir. “Just a ring.”

* * *

The engagement was in the newspapers by Tuesday.

*RATHORE HEIR TO WED BANDRA BOOKSELLER,* said one. *FROM STEEL TO STORIES,* said another, with a photo of them coming down the steps of the Malabar Hill house that someone must have taken with a very long lens. In the picture Tara was laughing at something and Kabir was looking at her, not at the camera, and his face was doing a thing it never did at work.

Zoya pinned the cutting to the noticeboard, directly on top of the eviction letter.

“Take that down,” said Tara.

“No.”

“Zoya.”

“It’s good for business,” said Zoya, which turned out to be true. On Tuesday, eleven new customers came in to look at the bookseller who was marrying a billionaire. On Wednesday it was thirty. On Thursday a woman from a lifestyle magazine asked if she could photograph the café for a feature called *Mumbai’s Most Romantic Corners*, and Tara said yes, because the roof still leaked and the photographer paid for a pot of chai and four slices of banana cake.

Kabir started coming in every evening at seven.

The first time, he said it was so they could be seen together. The second time, he said Iyer had told him it was good for the story. The third time he didn’t say anything at all, just walked to the table at the back with a paperback from the second-hand shelf and a cup of chai, and read until closing, while customers took his picture and Zoya sold them bookmarks.

On the fourth evening, Tara sat down across from him.

“What are you reading?”

He showed her. It was a battered copy of a detective novel with a skeleton on the cover.

“That’s my father’s,” she said. “Look, his name’s inside. He loved those. He used to guess the murderer by page forty and then sulk when he was right.”

Kabir opened the cover. In faded blue biro, in big loopy letters, it said *Property of Ashok Mehta. Return or face consequences.*

He laughed. He put his hand flat on the page, the way he had with the *Gitanjali*, careful.

“I’ll return it,” he said.

“Keep it,” said Tara. “He’d have liked you. He liked anyone who read at the table.”

* * *

On Friday he didn’t come.

At seven, Tara caught herself looking at the door. At seven-thirty she caught herself looking at it again. At eight she told Zoya she was going home early with a headache, and instead she bought six vada pav and two cutting chais from the stall at the station, wrapped them in newspaper, and took a taxi to Lower Parel.

The night guard at the Rathore Group tower recognised her from the newspapers, which was a strange feeling, and waved her through to the lift. On the forty-second floor, everything was dark except a single lamp in the corner office, and in its light Kabir sat at his enormous glass desk with his tie loosened and his head in his hands, surrounded by papers.

He looked up when she knocked on the open door. For a second he looked so purely, simply glad to see her that she nearly dropped the vada pav.

“You’re not supposed to be here,” he said.

“You’re not supposed to be here either. It’s Friday night.” She held up the newspaper parcel. “Have you eaten?”

He thought about it, which was answer enough.

They ate on the floor by the window, because the glass desk felt too much like a boardroom, with the newspaper spread out between them and the whole city lit up below like a spilled jewellery box. The vada pav were still hot. Kabir ate his first one in three bites and reached for a second with the expression of a man rediscovering something he had forgotten existed.

“When did you last eat one of these?” Tara asked.

“College.” He licked chutney off his thumb, which she was sure no one on the Rathore board had ever seen him do. “There was a stall outside the library. I used to buy two and eat them on the steps and read until the guard threw me out.”

“And now?”

“Now I have a chef,” he said, “who makes something called a deconstructed vada pav, which is a single potato sphere on a plate with three dots of sauce.” He shook his head. “It costs eleven hundred rupees and it is a crime.”

Tara laughed so hard that she spilled chai on the newspaper. They both looked at the stain, spreading slowly across a headline about the stock market, and then at each other.

“At least it wasn’t a first edition,” Kabir said.

“At least it wasn’t a first edition,” Tara agreed.

Later, when the city had gone quieter below them and the vada pav were gone, he told her what the papers on his desk were: the redevelopment plans for Sea Lane. He had asked for them that morning. Forty floors, a rooftop pool, a car park where her café used to be. He had been sitting with them for six hours, trying, he said, to understand how anyone could look at her father’s shop and only see a number.

“And?” she asked.

“And I used to be the kind of person who would,” said Kabir. He didn’t look at her when he said it. He looked out at the city, at all the glass towers, his own among them. “Before the rain.”

* * *

The gala was on Saturday: the Rathore Foundation’s annual charity dinner, in the ballroom of the Sea Palace Hotel, with three hundred guests, a string quartet, and the entire Rathore board.

“Vikram will be there,” Kabir said in the car. “So will half the people who decide whether he gets the chairmanship. They’ll be watching.”

“Then we give them something to watch.”

He looked across at her.

She was wearing the green sari. Zoya had done her hair, pinning it up with a silver clip shaped like a dragonfly that had belonged to Zoya’s grandmother, and she had borrowed lipstick from Mrs D’Souza, who had insisted on two shades, as always. On her finger, Ananya Rathore’s ring caught the light from the street every time they passed under a lamp.

Kabir didn’t say anything for so long that she began to worry about the lipstick.

“What?” she said. “Is it too much? Mrs D’Souza said red was classic.”

“It’s not too much,” he said.

“Then why are you looking at me like that?”

He turned to the window. “I’m practising,” he said. “For in front of witnesses.”
