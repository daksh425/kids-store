# Epilogue: One Year Later

The rain arrived in Bandra the way it always did: all at once, without asking.

Nobody at 14 Sea Lane minded.

The building had a new roof. It had new wiring, and new windows, and a new sign over the shop door, painted by hand, by the owner, with her tongue between her teeth, so that the ampersand came out lopsided, and she laughed and left it that way. *Chapter & Chai,* it said, *since 2004.*

Inside, the shop had grown into the empty ground floor next door. There was a children’s corner now, with beanbags and a reading tent and a shelf of picture books, and every Sunday afternoon at four o’clock a serious man in round reading glasses sat on a very small chair and read stories out loud to a crowd of five-year-olds. He did voices. He did a particularly good angry cow.

At the back of the café, in a glass cabinet with a small brass lock, was a faded blue *Gitanjali* with a stain across poem thirty-five. It was not for sale. Below it, on a little card in neat, typed-looking handwriting, it said: *The most expensive book in Mumbai. Ask the owner why.*

* * *

They were married in the library on Malabar Hill, in the room with the round window.

It was a small wedding, by Rathore standards, which meant only three hundred people, most of whom had to stand on the lawn and watch through the window. Tara wore the green of the sea before a storm, and Kabir wore navy blue, which she told him was very boring, and he said he had chosen it on purpose so that everyone would look at her instead.

Zoya was maid of honour. Iyer was best man. He gave a speech that lasted exactly ninety seconds, in which he described the groom as *a decisive person* and the bride as *a personality*, and received a standing ovation.

Afterwards, Tara noticed, Iyer did not go back to his seat. He went and stood beside Zoya at the edge of the lawn, very straight, with his hands behind his back, and when the rain began to spit he produced, from somewhere inside his immaculate jacket, a small folding umbrella, and held it over her head without a word.

It was shaped like a frog.

“Since when,” Tara whispered to Kabir, “has Iyer owned a frog umbrella?”

“Since March,” said Kabir. “He asked me where to buy one. I didn’t ask why. I am,” he added, with great dignity, “a cautious man.”

Across the lawn, Zoya looked up at the frog, and then at Iyer, and then she laughed, and slipped her arm through his, and Iyer’s face did something that Tara had never seen it do before, which was smile.

Vikram was not invited. He had moved to Dubai to run a hotel, and sent a very large, very tasteful flower arrangement, and a card that said only *You win, cousin.* Kabir kept the card. Tara used the vase for umbrellas.

Mrs D’Souza signed the guestbook twice.

And at the end of the ceremony, when the priest asked for the ring, Savitri Rathore stood up from her chair by the window, leaning on her stick, and walked very slowly across her daughter-in-law’s library, and took a small gold band with a single diamond in a ring of seed pearls out of the pocket of her sari, and put it into her grandson’s hand.

“Earned,” she said.

It fitted perfectly. It always had.

* * *

That evening, after the guests had gone, they sat together on the window seat with its new green cushions, the colour of the sea before a storm, and watched the lights come on along the bay.

“Can I ask you something?” said Tara.

“You’ve asked me eleven thousand things this year.”

“The day you came into my shop. In the rain. You were hiding from the photographers.”

“I was browsing.”

“You were hiding. Why my shop? There’s a juice bar right next door. There’s a linen boutique. There’s a temple.”

Kabir was quiet for a moment. Then he reached into the pocket of his very boring navy-blue jacket and took out a folded piece of paper, soft with age, and put it in her hand.

It was a photograph, torn from an old newspaper. A small bookshop with a hand-painted sign. A man with a big moustache standing in the doorway, laughing, holding up a detective novel with a skeleton on the cover. And beside him, aged about five, a girl with two plaits and a very serious expression, holding a glass of chai with both hands.

*NEW BOOKSHOP OPENS ON SEA LANE,* said the caption. *2004.*

“My mother cut it out,” Kabir said. “She was going to take me there. She said it looked like the kind of place where people read at the table.” He looked at the photograph in Tara’s hand. “She never got the chance. I found it in the library last year, in the cookbook, between biryani and the angry cow. And I remembered, that day in the rain, that it was on Sea Lane. So when I needed somewhere to hide—”

“Browse,” Tara said, through a very tight throat.

“—to *browse*,” he agreed, “I thought I’d go and see if she was right.”

Tara looked at the little girl in the picture, with her plaits and her serious face and her glass of chai held tight in both hands, and she started to laugh, and she started to cry, and she found she could do both at once after all.

“Was she?” she asked. “Right?”

Kabir took the photograph and tucked it carefully into the *Gitanjali*, between poem thirty-five and poem thirty-six, where it would be safe.

“Yes,” he said. “She usually was.”

Outside, the rain came down over the sea, and the city glittered, and on the top floor of a white house on a hill, in a library with a round window, two people who had once agreed not to fall in love sat reading at the table.

*The End*
